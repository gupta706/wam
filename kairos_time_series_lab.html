The world is a partially observed dynamical system.

We never see the whole world. We see measurements: satellite pixels, machine telemetry, electricity meters, GPS signals and more. The central question is what a model can infer from that incomplete history — and what that inference lets us do next.

If we can only observe a small part of a complex world, how much can a machine actually learn and predict?

01 — HIDDEN DYNAMICS · CASE STUDY

What is the hidden world in cyclone prediction?

Before a satellite image exists, the atmosphere already has a state. We cannot observe every pressure, wind, temperature and moisture value everywhere. The forecasting problem is therefore: infer a hidden dynamical state from partial, noisy observations and use that state to predict what will be observed next.

A — hidden state

What could xt contain?

For a pedagogical local cyclone model, let the hidden state be a compressed representation of the storm environment:

xt = [ Δpc, Vmax, Tcore, qmid, usteer, vsteer ]⊤

These numbers are illustrative state values, not a calibrated physical weather model. A real numerical weather model would have millions to billions of state variables on a spatial grid.

B — evolution

What could A, B, and wt mean?

In the local linear approximation

xt+1 = A xt + B ut + wt

A describes how the current atmospheric state propagates itself; B ut represents known external forcing/covariates; wt represents unresolved atmospheric dynamics and model error.

In a pure TSFM setting there is normally no controllable action. Thus we can set B ut = 0, or treat known weather covariates as inputs rather than actions. This is exactly why the paper's TSFM formulation focuses on the information available from observations.

yradar,t = hradar(xt) + εt
The key idea: the world is high-dimensional, while each sensor exposes only a projection of it. Two different atmospheric states can therefore produce very similar observations. Those directions are weakly identifiable or unobservable from that sensor configuration.
03 — HISTORY → INFORMATION STATE

What exactly are Ht and φθ(Ht)?

Ht = (y1, y2, …, yt)

Ht is the history available at forecast time t. For the cyclone example it could contain the last 24–72 hours of satellite images, radar fields, station observations and known covariates, sampled every 10 minutes, hourly, or at whatever cadence the model uses.

What does φθ do?

φθ is the learned encoder implemented by the TSFM. It reads the observation history and compresses the parts useful for prediction into a representation:

zt = φθ(Ht)

θ denotes learned model parameters. Conceptually, φθ may combine spatial patterns, temporal trends, lagged dependencies, cross-sensor relationships and uncertainty-relevant features.

Why call zt an information state?

The full history can be enormous. We call zt an approximate information state when it preserves the information from Ht that is relevant for predicting the future:

p(yt+1:t+K | Ht) ≈ p(yt+1:t+K | zt)

If this approximation is good, the model does not need to retain every pixel and every old measurement separately. The representation carries the predictive content of the history.

Important distinction: zt is not automatically an exact physical state such as pressure or wind. It is a learned representation. Calling it an approximate information state is a statement about its predictive sufficiency: it should retain what matters for the future, even if it does not reconstruct every hidden atmospheric variable.

Where Fisher information enters

Before the TSFM can learn a useful information state, the observation channel must contain information about the hidden dynamics. Locally,

Ix ≈ Jt⊤ Rt−1 Jt

Here Jt tells us which perturbations in the atmosphere change the sensor measurements, while Rt tells us how uncertain those measurements are. A direction invisible to the sensor produces little or no Fisher information. A direction measured precisely produces more information.

EQUATION-BY-EQUATION CASE STUDY

What each equation is saying about the cyclone

01

Hidden dynamics

xt+1 = A xt + B ut + wt

Start with the atmosphere itself. xt is the hidden state. A tells us how today's pressure, wind, moisture and temperature influence tomorrow's state. ut contains known external information such as sea-surface temperature or steering flow, while wt captures effects we have not modelled.

Question answered: If we could see the entire atmosphere, how would it evolve?
02

Observation physics

yt = h(xt) + εt

We do not observe xt directly. h(·) is the measurement rule. It can represent radiative transfer for a satellite, electromagnetic/radar physics for radar, or a direct measurement model for a station. εt is measurement and retrieval error.

Question answered: Given the true atmosphere, what would our instrument see?
03

Local sensitivity and Fisher information

Jt = ∂h/∂x     Ix,t ≈ Jt⊤Rt−1Jt

Jt asks: if I perturb the atmosphere slightly, how much does the measurement change? Rt describes measurement uncertainty. Thus Fisher information is high in state directions that strongly affect the observation and are measured precisely.

Question answered: Which hidden atmospheric changes can the sensors actually distinguish?
04

History

Ht = (y1, y2, …, yt)

A single satellite image is not the whole story. The temporal sequence tells us whether the storm is deepening, moving, rotating, weakening or changing structure. History turns isolated measurements into evidence about dynamics.

Question answered: What evidence has accumulated about the hidden process?
05

Learned information state

zt = φθ(Ht)

The TSFM maps the long, high-dimensional history into a learned representation. It can encode temporal patterns, spatial structure, sensor relationships and context. The key claim is not that zt equals pressure or wind; it is that zt can retain the information needed to predict the future.

Question answered: Can a learned representation summarize the predictive content of the entire history?
06

Approximate information-state property

p(yt+1:t+K|Ht) ≈ p(yt+1:t+K|zt)

This is the central conceptual bridge. If the two conditional distributions are close, then the detailed history contains little additional predictive information beyond zt. The TSFM has learned a compressed state that is sufficient for forecasting, even if it is not sufficient for reconstructing every physical variable.

Question answered: Has the model compressed the past without throwing away what matters for the future?
07

Forecast distribution

p(yt+1:t+K|zt)

The output is a distribution over possible futures. For a cyclone this could mean distributions over track, maximum wind, central pressure, rainfall or future satellite observations. Different futures can remain plausible because the underlying atmosphere is only partially observed.

Question answered: Given the information we currently have, which futures remain plausible?

The key idea: impact comes from turning incomplete measurements into a representation that is useful for the next decision — not from claiming that the model has reconstructed the entire world.

Why this matters

The model's impact is not magic. It is information.

When observations are incomplete, the useful question is not “Did the model see everything?” It is “Did the model extract enough predictive information from what it did see?”

What reaches the model

Coverage, noise, sampling and history determine how much evidence the model has about the hidden world.

What reaches the forecast

A stronger information state can support tighter near-term forecasts; uncertainty still grows as the future gets farther away.

Why time-series foundation models fit naturally

Long histories are not just data. They are evidence about a hidden process.

A time-series foundation model can be viewed as a learned mapping from heterogeneous histories to representations that support prediction. The world-model interpretation becomes useful when the representation behaves like an approximate information state.
